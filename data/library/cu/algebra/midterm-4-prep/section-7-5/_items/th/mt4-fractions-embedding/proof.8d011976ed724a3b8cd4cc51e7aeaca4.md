**The inclusion.** Use the operations and equality test in @[the ring of fractions]mt4-ring-of-fractions. For any $e\in D$, set $\iota(r)=re/e$. This is independent of $e$ because $(re)f=(rf)e$ for every $f\in D$. Adding two such fractions or multiplying them gives $\iota(r+s)$ or $\iota(rs)$, respectively, using denominator $e^2$. If $\iota(r)=\iota(s)$, cross multiplication gives $re^2=se^2$. Since $e^2\in D$ is nonzero and is not a zero divisor, @[cancellation]mt4-cancellation gives $r=s$. Thus $\iota$ is an injective @[ring homomorphism]mt4-ring-homomorphism.

**The denominators.** For $d\in D$, the product of $\iota(d)=de/e$ with $e/(de)$ is $(de^2)/(de^2)=1$. Thus $\iota(d)$ is a @[unit]mt4-unit. Fraction multiplication gives $\iota(d)(r/d)=\iota(r)$, so every fraction equals $\iota(r)\iota(d)^{-1}$.

**Extension and uniqueness.** A @[unital homomorphism preserves inverses]mt4-units-under-isomorphism, so the preceding identity forces any unital extension to send $r/d$ to $\phi(r)\phi(d)^{-1}$. Define $\Phi$ by this formula. If $r/d=s/e$, then $re=sd$, so $\phi(r)\phi(e)=\phi(s)\phi(d)$. Multiplying by the inverses of $\phi(e)$ and $\phi(d)$ proves that $\Phi$ is well defined. Conversely, equal images imply the same cross-product equality in $S$. The injectivity of $\phi$ then gives $re=sd$. Thus $\Phi$ is injective.

The addition and multiplication formulas for fractions are valid for the images in $S$ because their denominators are units, so $\Phi$ respects both operations. Also $\Phi(e/e)=1$ and $\Phi(re/e)=\phi(r)$, proving that it preserves the identity and extends $\phi$. The forced formula gives uniqueness.
