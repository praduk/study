**Existence.** First prove the @[ascending chain condition]math:algebra:factorization-noetherian:df:noetherian. For ideals $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$, their union $I$ satisfies the @[ideal test]mt4-ideal. It contains zero, any two elements have their difference in a common member, and ring multiples stay in the member that contains the given element. Since $R$ is a @[PID]mt4-pid, write $I=(d)$. Some $I_n$ contains $d$, and then $(d)\subseteq I_n\subseteq I=(d)$. Every later term also equals $I$.

If a nonzero nonunit $a_1$ had no finite irreducible factorization, it could not itself be @[irreducible]mt4-irreducible. Thus $a_1=a_2b$ for two nonunits, and at least one factor, say $a_2$, also has no finite irreducible factorization. Since $a_2\ne0$, we have $(a_1)\subsetneq(a_2)$: equality would give $a_2=ra_1=ra_2b$, whence $rb=1$ by @[cancellation]mt4-cancellation. Repeating the selection (using @[dependent choice]math:set:axiom-choice:rk:choice-dependencies) produces a strictly increasing sequence of principal ideals, contradicting stabilization. Hence a finite factorization exists.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose two factorizations of the same element are given. The first irreducible $p$ on one side is a @[prime element in a PID]mt4-pid-prime-irreducible. Applying the @[prime element property]mt4-prime-element repeatedly, $p$ divides some irreducible $q$ on the other side. In $q=pc$, the factor $p$ is a nonunit, so $c$ is a unit. After reordering the second factorization, cancel $p$ from both sides and absorb $c$ into the leading unit. Repeat. When one side runs out of irreducibles, the other has none left either: a product equal to a unit forces each factor to have an inverse (if $xy=u$ is a unit, $yu^{-1}$ inverts $x$). Thus the lengths agree and corresponding factors are @[associates]mt4-associates, as required for @[unique factorization]mt4-ufd.

Finally every Euclidean domain is a PID by @[Proposition 1]mt4-euclidean-principal, so it too is a UFD.
