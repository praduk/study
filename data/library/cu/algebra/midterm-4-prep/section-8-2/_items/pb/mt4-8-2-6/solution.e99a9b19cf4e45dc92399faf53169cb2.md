**(a)** Order the nonprincipal ideals by inclusion. The union $U$ of any nonempty chain satisfies the @[ideal test]mt4-ideal. It contains $0$, any two of its elements lie in a common member and have their difference there, and ring multiples stay in that member. If $U=(u)$, some member $K$ would contain $u$. Then $(u)\subseteq K\subseteq U=(u)$, making $K$ principal, a contradiction. Thus the union is an upper bound in this poset. The empty chain has an upper bound by the nonemptiness assumption. By @[Zorn’s lemma]mt4-zorn-lemma, there is a maximal member $I$. It is proper since $R=(1)$, and it cannot be prime by hypothesis. The definition of @[a prime ideal]mt4-prime-ideal now gives $a,b\notin I$ with $ab\in I$.

**(b)** Since $a\notin I$, the @[ideal sum]mt4-ideal-sum-product $I_a=I+(a)$ strictly contains $I$. Maximality among nonprincipal ideals makes $I_a$ principal, say $(\alpha)$. The set $J$ contains $0$ and is an ideal since $(r-s)y=ry-sy$ and $(tr)y=t(ry)$ for $r,s\in J$, $y\in I_a$, $t\in R$. Every $r\in I$ satisfies $rI_a\subseteq I$. Also $bI_a=bI+(ba)\subseteq I$, so $b\in J$ and $I\subsetneq I_b\subseteq J$. Hence $J=(\beta)$ by maximality. By @[the definition of an ideal product]mt4-ideal-sum-product, $I_aJ=(\alpha\beta)$: products of multiples of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are multiples of $\alpha\beta$, and $\alpha\beta$ itself lies in the product. Its generators lie in $I$ by the definition of $J$, so $I_aJ\subseteq I$.

**(c)** Given $x\in I\subseteq I_a=(\alpha)$, write $x=s\alpha$. Then $sI_a=(s\alpha)=(x)\subseteq I$, so $s\in J$. Thus $x\in I_aJ$, and (b) gives $I=I_aJ=(\alpha\beta)$. This contradicts the choice of $I$. Every ideal is therefore principal, as required by @[the definition of a PID]mt4-pid.
