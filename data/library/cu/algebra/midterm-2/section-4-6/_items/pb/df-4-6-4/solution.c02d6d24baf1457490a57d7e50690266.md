Each $3$-cycle is a product of two transpositions, so it is even. Hence the subgroup $L$ generated by all $3$-cycles satisfies $L\le A_n$.

For the reverse inclusion, take $\sigma\in A_n$. Since transpositions generate $S_n$, write $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions. Evenness says that the number of factors is even, so group them in consecutive pairs. It is enough to express each pair as a product of $3$-cycles.

There are three possibilities:

1. If the transpositions are equal, their product is the identity and contributes no $3$-cycles.
2. If they share exactly one point, after naming that point $a$ the pair is $(a\ b)(a\ c)=(a\ c\ b)$, a $3$-cycle.
3. If they are disjoint, then for four distinct points,

   $$
   (a\ b)(c\ d)=(a\ c\ b)(a\ c\ d).
   $$

   To verify the last identity, apply the right side from right to left: it sends $a\mapsto b$, $b\mapsto a$, $c\mapsto d$, and $d\mapsto c$, and fixes all other points.

Thus every pair belongs to $L$, and their product $\sigma$ does too. Therefore $A_n\le L$, proving $A_n=L$. When $n=3$ the disjoint case simply cannot occur, and the same argument still works.

**Technique:** pair transpositions because the sign forces an even count. This generation theorem does not require the much stronger simplicity theorem.
