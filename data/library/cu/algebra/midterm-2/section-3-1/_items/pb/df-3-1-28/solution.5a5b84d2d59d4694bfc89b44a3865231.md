**Forward implication.** If $g\in N_G(N)$, then $gNg^{-1}=N$. Since $S\subseteq N$, every $gsg^{-1}$ belongs to $N$.

**Reverse implication: first obtain inclusion.** Suppose $gsg^{-1}\in N$ for every $s\in S$. Any element $n\in N$ is a finite word $s_1^{\epsilon_1}\cdots s_k^{\epsilon_k}$ with $s_i\in S$ and $\epsilon_i\in\{1,-1\}$. Conjugation preserves multiplication and inverses, so

$$
gng^{-1}=(gs_1g^{-1})^{\epsilon_1}\cdots(gs_kg^{-1})^{\epsilon_k}\in N.
$$

This also covers the identity, represented by the empty word. Thus $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$.

**Use the finite hypothesis.** The map $n\mapsto gng^{-1}$ is a bijection $N\to gNg^{-1}$ with inverse conjugation by $g^{-1}$. Consequently $|gNg^{-1}|=|N|$. An inclusion between finite sets of equal cardinality is equality, so $gNg^{-1}=N$.

**Why finiteness matters.** In $\GL_2(\Q)$ let $N=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&m\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right):m\in\Z\}$, generated by the element with $m=1$, and let $g=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$. Then conjugation sends $m$ to $2m$. The generator is sent into $N$, but the conjugate subgroup contains only even upper-right entries and is proper in $N$.

**Technique:** “generators map into the subgroup” proves inclusion; a separate argument is needed for equality.
