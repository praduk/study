**Use when:** the matrix pattern is fixed, especially Heisenberg or upper triangular groups.

Write $X(a,b,c)=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\0&1&c\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and derive the coordinate multiplication law once. Solve the identity equation for the inverse. Compute powers inductively using this same law. To exhibit noncommutativity choose coordinates so one cross term is $1$ and the reverse cross term is $0$.

Over $\F_2$ there are eight possible triples, and arithmetic is modulo two. Over $\R$, a positive integer $m$ is nonzero, so $ma=0$ implies $a=0$; this is why the infinite-order argument works there.

**Assigned uses:** 1.4.5, 11(a,b,e); 2.1.9; 2.4.12.

**Common failure:** carrying real-number reasoning into characteristic two, or assuming a displayed identity power is the exact order without excluding smaller powers.
