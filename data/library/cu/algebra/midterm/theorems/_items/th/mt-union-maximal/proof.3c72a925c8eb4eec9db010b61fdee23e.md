If $G=H\cup K$ with neither subgroup containing the other, choose $h\in H\setminus K$ and $k\in K\setminus H$. If $hk\in H$, then $k\in H$; if $hk\in K$, then $h\in K$. Both are contradictions. If one contains the other, the union is a proper subgroup and again cannot be $G$.
In the finite case, among proper subgroups containing a given $H<G$, one of largest cardinality is maximal. If $G$ were noncyclic, every $\langle g\rangle$ would be proper, putting every element in some maximal subgroup. At most two such subgroups cannot cover $G$. The trivial group is cyclic and is handled separately.
