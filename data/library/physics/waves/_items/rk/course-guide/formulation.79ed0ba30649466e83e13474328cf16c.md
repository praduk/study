Assume the Mathematics curriculum, especially differential equations, Fourier methods, complex numbers, and asymptotic reasoning. Physics familiarity is developed here: a wave is a propagating disturbance of an observable, not necessarily motion of matter with the wave. Read the mechanical model first, then spectral propagation, interfaces, interference, diffraction, ray optics, and polarization. Classical Mechanics and Continuum Mechanics supply useful physical examples, but the wave and optical premises used here are stated locally.

Use SI: wavelength and displacement in metres, time in seconds, frequency $f$ in hertz, angular frequency $\omega=2\pi f$ in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and wave number $k=2\pi/\lambda$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. The phasor convention is $\operatorname{Re}(Ue^{-i\omega t})$, so $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ with $k,\omega>0$ moves toward increasing $x$. Electric-field amplitudes are in volts per metre, intensity in watts per square metre. Mathematical prerequisites include @math:diffeq:boundary-fourier:df:fourier-coefficients. Every physical propagation or detector model is conditional; theorem entries establish consequences of the stated assumptions.

Inspected authorities: Howard Georgi's [The Physics of Waves](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/ef731c1b91d77a6db003f6c27e300d25_MIT8_03SCF16_Textbook.pdf), especially the chapters on signals, polarization, and diffraction; the [MIT 8.03 course syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/syllabus/) as a scope check; and Nick Fang's [MIT geometrical-optics notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-71-optics-spring-2014/d558a4fabb2cc2eee8f864d3928b31f6_MIT2_71S14_lec2_notes.pdf). The mathematical derivations and exercises here are original. Scalar paraxial optics does not replace the full electromagnetic boundary problem. Vector diffraction, partial-coherence theory beyond the basic correlation, nonlinear optics, and detailed aberration design are beyond this course.

The intrinsic route uses @physics:waves:geometry:df:phase-space-rays, the optical metric in @physics:waves:geometry:th:optical-metric, and the conserved flux form in @physics:waves:geometry:th:amplitude-flux. Phase, rays, and intensity transport then become cotangent-bundle and differential-form constructions. The exact punctured-space solution in @physics:waves:geometry:pb:spherical-ray-tube makes the approximation's geometric boundary concrete.
