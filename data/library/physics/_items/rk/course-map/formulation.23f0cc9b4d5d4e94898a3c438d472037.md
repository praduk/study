This curriculum assumes familiarity with the material under @math:rk:course-map, including graduate algebra, analysis, topology, and differential geometry. The emphasis is the construction and interpretation of physical models: what a state represents, what is measured, which laws are empirical inputs, and where a calculation is valid.

**Start:** @physics:foundations:rk:course-guide establishes units, conventions, approximation regimes, and the distinction between a model and a theorem.

**Core courses**

1. @physics:classical:rk:course-guide - Newtonian models, variational and Hamiltonian mechanics, symmetry, central forces, rigid bodies, and small oscillations.
2. @physics:waves:rk:course-guide - propagation, normal modes, dispersion, interference, diffraction, polarization, and geometrical optics.
3. @physics:electromagnetism:rk:course-guide - fields and sources, potentials and boundary problems, Maxwell dynamics, energy and momentum, media, and radiation.
4. @physics:thermo:rk:course-guide - equilibrium states, heat and work, entropy, thermodynamic potentials, response, and phase equilibrium.
5. @physics:statistical:rk:course-guide - ensembles, partition functions, fluctuations, quantum statistics, collective behavior, and thermodynamic limits.
6. @physics:relativity:rk:course-guide - Lorentzian spacetime, causality, clocks, four-momentum, relativistic dynamics, and covariant electromagnetism.
7. @physics:quantum:rk:course-guide - states and measurement, unitary dynamics, spectral problems, spin, approximation, scattering, and composite systems.
8. @physics:continuum:rk:course-guide - deformation, stress, local balances, fluids, elasticity, acoustics, and heat transport.
9. @physics:gravitation:rk:course-guide - spacetime geometry as a physical field, Einstein dynamics, Schwarzschild geometry, observational consequences, gravitational waves, and cosmology.
10. @physics:fields:rk:course-guide - classical field actions and currents, free-field quantization, spinors and gauge connections, perturbative calculations, and effective field theory.

**A reading route.** After foundations and mechanics, waves and electromagnetism form one branch and thermodynamics forms another. Read special relativity before general relativity. Read quantum mechanics before the quantum-statistics parts of statistical mechanics. Continuum mechanics extends classical mechanics to materials. Field theory draws on classical mechanics, relativity, electromagnetism, and quantum mechanics. Topic order is curricular; source references and course links also support revisiting earlier material.

**How to use the entries.** Physical laws and model postulates use Axiom entries, with their status explicit. Mathematical consequences use Theorem entries with separate proofs. Problems ask for a prediction, derivation, or diagnostic and have separate worked solutions. Remarks explain interpretation and limits. Knowing the prerequisite mathematics does not replace learning physical intuition or gaining laboratory experience.

**Scope.** These are substantial introductory course cores for mathematically mature readers. Atomic, particle, cosmological, and many-body ideas appear where their prerequisite models become available; specialized experimental methods, plasma physics, nuclear structure, advanced condensed matter, and quantum gravity are not complete courses here. Guides identify inspected author or university sources for further study and flag formal or source-dependent material.
