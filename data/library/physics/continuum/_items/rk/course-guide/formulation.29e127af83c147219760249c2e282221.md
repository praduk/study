The Mathematics curriculum is assumed; the new work is identifying what a field measures and choosing a physical model. Begin with material motion, then derive local balance equations before closing them with constitutive laws. Fluids, elastic solids, sound, and heat illustrate how the same calculus produces different predictions when the physical closure changes. The continuum idealization replaces microscopic particles by fields averaged over a scale small compared with the variation scale but large compared with microstructure.

We use SI and three spatial dimensions unless a reduced problem says otherwise. Density is $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, velocity $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, stress and pressure pascals, and temperature kelvins. Write $(\nabla v)_{ij}=\partial_jv_i$, $(\operatorname{div}\sigma)_i=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}$, and $A:B=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}B_{ij}$. Tension is positive stress; a positive hydrostatic pressure has stress $-p\mathbf1$. Fields and material maps are smooth in theorem entries, with sufficient boundary regularity for transport and divergence theorems. These hypotheses exclude shocks unless explicitly discussed.

Mathematical prerequisites include @math:diffgeo:stokes-cohomology:th:stokes and the PDE examples in the Differential Equations course. Mechanics supplies physical mass and force; thermodynamic constitutive detail is developed separately. No proof of global existence for three-dimensional Navier–Stokes, no turbulence closure, and no general nonlinear elasticity existence theorem is claimed.

The geometric route is @physics:continuum:geometry:df:material-forms, @physics:continuum:geometry:th:kelvin-circulation, and @physics:continuum:geometry:th:cauchy-vorticity. Deformation is a pulled-back metric; mass and vorticity are transported forms. The proofs here are direct smooth-field calculations. For broader geometric context, the inspected author-hosted paper by Khesin, Misiołek, and Modin, [Geometric Hydrodynamics of Compressible Fluids](https://www.math.toronto.edu/khesin/papers/newton_diffeos.pdf), treats the one-form Euler equation in Appendix A. No infinite-dimensional existence or geodesic-completeness result is assumed from that viewpoint.
