The first condition immediately implies the second by restricting any model. For the converse, suppose every finite subset is satisfiable. If $\Gamma$ is finite, it is itself one of those subsets. Otherwise, formulas are finite strings over a countable symbol stock, so enumerate $\Gamma=\{G_0,G_1,\ldots\}$ and the atoms as $p_0,p_1,\ldots$.

Build a binary tree $T$. A node at level $n$ is an assignment to $p_0,\ldots,p_{n-1}$ that can be extended to a total valuation satisfying $G_0,\ldots,G_{n-1}$. Every level is nonempty because that finite formula set is satisfiable, and every prefix of a node is again a node. A finitely branching tree with nodes at arbitrarily large levels has an infinite branch: starting at the root, at least one of its finitely many children has descendants at arbitrarily large levels; choose such a child and repeat.

Let $v$ be the total valuation read from this branch. Fix $G_k$ and choose $n>k$ large enough that every atom of $G_k$ occurs among $p_0,\ldots,p_{n-1}$. The branch node at level $n$ has an extension $w$ satisfying $G_0,\ldots,G_{n-1}$, hence $G_k$. Valuations $v$ and $w$ agree on every atom of $G_k$, so locality gives $v\models G_k$. Since $k$ was arbitrary, $v\models\Gamma$.

For the consequence form, if $\Gamma\models A$, then $\Gamma\cup\{\neg A\}$ is unsatisfiable. By the just-proved satisfiability form, some finite subset $D$ is unsatisfiable. Let $\Gamma_0=D\cap\Gamma$. Then $\Gamma_0\cup\{\neg A\}$ is unsatisfiable, whether or not $D$ already contained $\neg A$. Hence $\Gamma_0\models A$. The converse is immediate because any model of $\Gamma$ is a model of $\Gamma_0$.
