If $s$ exists, the map $(l,n)\mapsto i(l)+s(n)$ is surjective because $m-s(q(m))\in\ker q=\im i$. It is injective: applying $q$ to a zero value gives $n=0$, then injectivity of $i$ gives $l=0$. This proves (3). Conversely the product decomposition provides both $r$ and $s$. If $r$ exists, every $m$ is $i(r(m))+(m-i(r(m)))$, with the second summand in $\ker r$ and trivial intersection with $\im i$. The restriction $q|_{\ker r}$ is injective and surjective by this decomposition, so its inverse is the required section.
