A finite extension is generated by finitely many elements (a vector-space basis suffices), so it is enough to combine $K(\alpha,\beta)$. In a field where their minimal polynomials split, list the distinct conjugates $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_j$, with $\alpha_1=\alpha,\beta_1=\beta$. Choose nonzero $c\in K$ avoiding the finitely many values for which $\alpha_i+c\beta_j=\alpha+c\beta$ with $\beta_j\ne\beta$. This is possible because $K$ is infinite. Set $\theta=\alpha+c\beta$.

Over $K(\theta)$, the @[minimal polynomial]math:algebra:fields-galois:df:field-extension of $\beta$ over $K$ and the polynomial $m_\alpha(\theta-cx)$ have precisely one common root, namely $\beta$. Their roots are simple, so their monic gcd in the @[splitting field]math:algebra:fields-galois:df:separable-normal is $x-\beta$. Computing a monic gcd by the Euclidean algorithm over $K(\theta)$ shows its coefficients lie in that field. Therefore $\beta\in K(\theta)$, and $\alpha=\theta-c\beta$ lies there too. Combine generators inductively. Splitting fields needed here exist by adjoining a root through $K[x]/(f)$ for irreducible factors and repeating until the finite list of polynomials splits.
