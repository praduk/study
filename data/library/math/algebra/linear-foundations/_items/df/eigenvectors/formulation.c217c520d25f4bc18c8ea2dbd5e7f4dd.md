Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear endomorphism over a field $K$. A scalar $\lambda\in K$ is an eigenvalue when $Tv=\lambda v$ for some nonzero eigenvector $v$. Its eigenspace is $\ker(T-\lambda I)$. The zero vector belongs to every eigenspace but is never an eigenvector. The operator is diagonalizable over $K$ when $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors.

In finite dimension, the geometric multiplicity is $\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)$ and the algebraic multiplicity is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the @math:algebra:linear-foundations:df:determinant-trace @[characteristic polynomial]math:algebra:linear-foundations:df:determinant-trace. A generalized eigenvector satisfies $(T-\lambda I)^kv=0$ for some positive integer $k$, with $v\ne0$. These definitions do not assert that geometric and algebraic multiplicities are equal.

The real rotation through $\pi/2$ has no real eigenvalue: its characteristic polynomial is $t^2+1$. Over $\C$ its two eigenvalues are $i$ and $-i$. The field is therefore part of an eigenvalue or diagonalizability assertion. The spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator can contain values that are not eigenvalues; see @math:analysis:spectral-theory:df:spectrum-compact.
