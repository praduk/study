A bilinear form on a @[vector space]math:algebra:linear-foundations:df:vector-space $V$ over a field $K$ is a map $b:V\times V\to K$ linear separately in each argument. It is symmetric if $b(v,w)=b(w,v)$, alternating if $b(v,v)=0$ for every $v$, and nondegenerate if both radicals vanish: $b(v,w)=0$ for every $w$ forces $v=0$, and $b(v,w)=0$ for every $v$ forces $w=0$. In finite dimension nondegeneracy is equivalent to invertibility of a representing matrix. Over characteristic two, alternating and skew-symmetric must not be conflated.

For a symmetric real bilinear form, $q(v)=b(v,v)$ is its quadratic form, and $b(v,w)=(q(v+w)-q(v)-q(w))/2$. Positive definite means $q(v)>0$ for every nonzero $v$; positive semidefinite means $q(v)\ge0$ for every $v$. The form $q(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ is nondegenerate but indefinite. The form $q(x,y)=x^2$ is positive semidefinite but degenerate.

A Hermitian form on a complex space satisfies $h(v,w)=\overline{h(w,v)}$ and is linear in one argument and conjugate-linear in the other. Mathematics uses linearity in the first argument, as in @math:analysis:hilbert-spaces:df:hilbert; Physics uses the opposite convention explicitly. Positive definiteness gives an @[inner product]math:analysis:hilbert-spaces:df:hilbert; completeness is a separate condition. A matrix is Hermitian when its conjugate transpose equals itself, regardless of this argument-order convention.
