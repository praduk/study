Compactness implies total boundedness by the cover of all radius-$\varepsilon$ balls. It implies sequential compactness: if a sequence had no cluster point, every point would have a neighborhood containing only finitely many terms (counting indices); a finite subcover would then contain only finitely many terms in total. A cluster point supplies a subsequence using radius-$1/n$ balls. A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit, so compactness implies completeness.

Completeness and total boundedness imply sequential compactness: successively select infinite subsequences lying in single balls of radii $2^{-k}$; a diagonal subsequence is Cauchy and converges.

Finally assume sequential compactness. Failure of total boundedness yields an infinite sequence of mutually $\varepsilon$-separated points, impossible. Every open cover has a Lebesgue number: otherwise choose $x_n$ whose radius-$1/n$ ball fits in no cover member; a convergent subsequence and a neighborhood of its limit contradict this property. A finite cover by balls smaller than that Lebesgue number therefore refines to finitely many members of the given cover. Hence $X$ is compact.
