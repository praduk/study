First prove the fresh-constant lemma: if a constant $c$ occurs in neither $Σ$ nor $ψ(x)$ and $Σ⊢ψ(c)$, choose a variable $y$ absent from the entire finite derivation after alpha-renaming bound variables if necessary. Uniformly replace $c$ by $y$. This preserves every rule instance without capture, and $y$ is free in no member of $Σ$; universal introduction therefore gives $Σ⊢∀yψ(y)$.

Build languages $L_0⊆L_1⊆…$ and theories $T_0⊆T_1⊆…$, starting with $L_0=L$ and $T_0=T$. For every formula $φ(x)$ of $L_n$ with at most $x$ free, allocate a distinct new constant $c_φ$ not in $L_n$. Well-order these formulas and add their sentences $h_φ=(∃xφ(x)→φ(c_φ))$ one at a time. Each addition preserves consistency. Otherwise, for the theory $Σ$ before that addition, deduction would give $Σ⊢¬h_φ$, hence $Σ⊢∃xφ(x)$ and $Σ⊢¬φ(c_φ)$. The fresh-constant lemma gives $Σ⊢∀x¬φ(x)$, contradicting $Σ⊢∃xφ(x)$. At limit points in the well-order, consistency follows from finite character. Let $L_{n+1}$ add all constants allocated at stage $n$, and let $T_{n+1}$ contain the resulting witness sentences.

Set $L^H=⋃_{n<ω}L_n$ and $T^H=⋃_{n<ω}T_n$. A contradiction would use finitely many assumptions and hence occur in one consistent stage. Every $L^H$-formula uses finitely many symbols, so it already belongs to some $L_n$ and receives a witness constant at stage $n+1$. Thus $T^H$ has the Henkin property.
